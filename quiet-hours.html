(02) — Context

A wellbeing app
that refuses to
feel like a chore
.

Most focus and screen-time apps rely on guilt. Streaks, leaderboards, "you used your phone 4 hours today" — engagement loops borrowed from the very products they're protecting users from.

The Quiet Hours bet: the calmest app you'll have on your phone. No streaks, no scores, no notifications outside of the ones you opted into. Just well-designed time, repeated.

(01)
Anti-streak philosophy

No gamification. Skipping a day is fine. Quiet Hours protects time, it doesn't score it.

Trust over telemetry

On-device by default. Your focus data never leaves the phone. Sold this as a feature, not a constraint.

Calm by construction

(03) — The Problem

Wellbeing apps
are usually the
loudest things
on the phone
.

In early research, six of eight participants had a "wellbeing graveyard" — folders of focus, meditation, and screen-time apps they'd uninstalled or muted. The pattern was consistent: install during a moment of intent, abandon when the app started demanding the very attention it was supposed to protect.

The product opportunity wasn't a better feature set — it was a calmer one.

6/8

Failure mode 01
Streak guilt

Users described feeling worse, not better, after missing a day. The streak became the thing being protected, not the focus.

Failure mode 02
Notification creep

Apps that opted users into push by default, or buried opt-outs in settings. The first thing the user did was disable the app.

Failure mode 03
Visual loudness

Bright gradients, achievement badges, oversized progress rings. Users said "the app stresses me out" — the antithesis of the value prop.

Deep Dive
The Quiet Block

One screen.
Many opinions.
Zero noise
.

The Quiet Block is the central interaction — a protected window of focus time. Most focus apps add controls (extend, pause, edit goal); we removed them. Once a block starts, the user has exactly one option: stop. Everything else is read-only.

This required hard product decisions: no extending, no pausing, no in-block editing. Friction-by-design. The screen during a block looks almost empty. That emptiness is the feature.

The research
8 USER INTERVIEWS — Pre-build
20 DIARY-STUDY DAYS — Across 5 testers
TESTFLIGHT — 12 weeks, 80 testers
BEHAVIOURAL DATA — On-device only
01
Removable, not editable

A running block can be stopped. It cannot be edited, extended, or paused. The simplicity removes negotiation with yourself, which is where focus apps usually lose users.

Trade-off → some users wanted "extend by 5 min". We held the line. Retention was higher with the simpler version.
02
Calm motion, not absent motion

Animations are slow, soft, and asymmetric. The block edge breathes — barely. Static feels lifeless; conventional motion feels demanding. We landed on motion you barely notice unless you look.

Outcome → reviewers consistently called the app "calming" — verbatim, in App Store reviews.
03
Sound design as a feature

Each block has an optional ambient sound, designed in collaboration with a composer. Loops are non-repeating. Volume defaults to imperceptible.

Trade-off → audio added 2 weeks of engineering time. It's the most-mentioned feature in 5★ reviews.

(09) — Reflection

What I'd do
differently.

What worked
Saying no to category conventions

Every choice that worked started as "what if we just didn't". No streaks, no push, no edit-during-block. The product's identity is in those subtractions.

Sound as a first-class material

Treating audio as a core design surface, not a polish item, was the single most-loved decision. Sound design is underrated in product design.

Whiteboard-first, Figma-second

Working physically with the engineer kept the loop tight. Specs only existed when something needed to be re-explained.

What I'd do differently
Brand work earlier

I let positioning slip until month 2. The first round of UI was generic because we didn't yet have a voice. Brand should've been week 1.

Plan for the Apple feature

Being featured caught us flat-footed. I'd invest in store assets, video, and onboarding load-tests as if a feature were coming, every time.

Test the no-streak hypothesis harder

I had conviction but limited evidence. With more rigorous A/B I could have made the differentiator harder to copy.
