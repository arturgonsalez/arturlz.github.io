(02) — Context

The single source
of truth for
enterprise capex

If you've ever seen how big companies plan capital spending, it's mostly spreadsheets, email threads, and a SharePoint folder nobody fully trusts. Finario is the tool a lot of those teams have moved to instead. Budgets, approvals, forecasts, reporting, all in one place, sitting on top of whatever ERP they already run.

It's used in 70+ countries across all kinds of industries, from manufacturing to energy to consumer goods. The same screens have to work for a $5,000 line item and for projects worth several hundred million. So the product has a lot of depth: currencies, fiscal years, NPV, IRR, work breakdowns, the whole finance vocabulary is built in.

(03) — The Problem

A powerful platform,
asking too much
of the people
using it

When I joined, the product worked. Customers were getting real value out of it. The problem was that it had been built and added to for over a decade, and you could feel that on every screen. Buttons everywhere, every option visible at once, hierarchies that made sense to whoever shipped that feature in 2017.

It was great if you used it every day. If you only logged in once a month to approve something or check a forecast, which is most users honestly, it was a lot. The job wasn't to dumb anything down. It was to make the same product feel obvious to the people who didn't live in it.

10+ yrs

of features layered on top of each other,
without breaking what power users rely on
Failure mode 01
Capability over clarity

No clear primary action on most screens. New users couldn't tell what they were supposed to click. Veterans were running on muscle memory.

Failure mode 02
Inconsistent patterns

Submitting, approving, forecasting all worked slightly differently depending on where you were in the app. Each area had been designed in its own time.

Failure mode 03
No system, no scale

No shared components, no tokens, no agreed patterns. Every new feature meant re-deciding what a button or a table should look like.

"This is where our financial truth lives. It should look like it knows that."

— Customer call, early 2024

Deep Dive
Platform Redesign

Keep the depth.
Lower the floor

First thing I did was put every screen of the product on one big board and just look at it. Investment forms, approval queues, forecast tables, dashboards, reports. What I was looking for wasn't what made each one different. It was what they all had in common.

Pretty quickly it was clear: every screen is doing some mix of the same five things. Help you orient, show you data, let you filter it, let you act on it, let you report on it. The screens didn't agree on how to do any of those. Getting them to agree was where most of the wins came from.

The approach
AUDIT — Every screen in the product
INTERVIEWS — Customers across 6+ industries
PAIRING — Daily with engineering
VALIDATION — On every customer call I could
01
A shared layout grammar

Header for context, main work area, side panel for related stuff, sticky toolbar at the bottom. Same idea everywhere. Once you've used one screen, you know how the others work.

Trade-off: long-time users had to relearn a few of their favourite layouts. We rolled it out gradually so the parity was visible.
02
Investment as the unit

An investment is the thing Finario was always about. Approvals, forecasts and reports are all just different views into one. Once we built around that idea, the product stopped feeling like five separate apps stitched together.

Result: fewer screens, more depth per screen. People stopped getting lost in submenus.
03
Design system before features

I refused to ship a single redesigned screen until the basics were in place. Tokens, the type system, the core components. Q1 looked slow from the outside. Everything after Q1 was way faster.

Trade-off: needed leadership to trust me through that first quarter. Paid back several times over.

(09) — Reflection

What I'd do
differently.

What worked
Doing the system first

It felt slow at the time. Looking back, it's the single best decision I made. Everything after that quarter was noticeably faster, and the product started feeling consistent without me having to police it.

Being in customer calls

Hearing customers directly is just different from reading a Notion summary. The way someone says "yeah, it's fine" when it clearly isn't, that doesn't survive a second-hand telling.

Working really closely with engineering

I'm in Slack with the engineers all day. We catch things together that would have been a back-and-forth otherwise. The closer the loop, the better the thing that ships.

What I'd do differently
Document as I go

I shipped patterns way faster than I wrote them down. As the team grows that gap is going to start hurting. If I were starting over I'd write the docs at the same time, not later.

Set up some design metrics earlier

I spent year one on craft and shipping. I'd now love to be able to point at adoption and quality numbers. Easier to defend the work, easier to spot what's not landing.

Write the vision down

A lot of where the product is going lives in my head and in conversations. Putting it on paper would let other people pull in the same direction without having to ask me every time.
