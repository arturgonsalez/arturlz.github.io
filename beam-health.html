(02) — Context

The screen between
a doctor and
their next patient
.

Charting is the most-used screen in any EHR-adjacent product. A clinician sees 20–30 patients a day; each visit ends with charting. Save thirty seconds per chart and you give a clinician back an hour.

Beam's existing charting flow had grown by accretion — a dropdown here, a modal there — until it was a 14-field form a clinician had to navigate while talking to a patient or in the 12-second handoff between rooms.

(01)
High-stakes UX

A bad chart isn't a bad form fill — it's a missing diagnosis or a billing error. Errors carry clinical and financial weight.

(02)
Compliance is the floor

HIPAA, audit logging, and clinical-content rules constrain every interaction. Design without legal in the room is design that gets rolled back.

(03)
Twelve-second windows

Clinicians don't have minutes to chart. They have seconds, often standing, often interrupted. The interaction model has to assume hostile conditions.

(03) — The Problem

A 14-field form,
in 12 seconds,
while standing
.

Chart-completion time was the company's most-watched non-clinical metric, and it had been creeping up for three releases. Diagnosis was hard: clinicians completed charts late at night, in batches, off the floor. The screen was failing them — but quietly, and asynchronously.

Shadowing 14 clinicians across 4 specialties surfaced a sharper picture: the charting screen demanded full attention at the moment clinicians had the least to give.

8.2 min
Average chart-completion time
(category baseline: 4.5 min)
Failure mode 01
Modal interruptions

Required-field errors fired as modals mid-charting, breaking train of thought. Clinicians described "starting over" mentally after each one.

Failure mode 02
Hidden defaults

Smart defaults existed but were buried two levels deep. Most clinicians didn't know they existed; they typed everything from scratch.

Failure mode 03
Audit-shaped UI

The form was structured for the auditor, not the clinician. Order of fields matched the chart's legal structure, not the encounter's natural flow.

"I chart at 9pm at home. The screen is the last thing I see before I close the laptop and the first thing I dread in the morning."

— Internist, shadowing study

Deep Dive
Encounter-first form

Field order is a
design decision
.

The single highest-impact change was reordering the form. The original chart was structured for the auditor reading it three months later — diagnosis codes first, history last. Clinicians filled it in a different order: history → observations → assessment → plan, mirroring the encounter itself.

Every clinician we shadowed scrolled up and down to fill the form, because the form's structure didn't match how they thought. Reordering looked trivial in Figma. It required four months of compliance review.

The research
14 SHADOWING SESSIONS — 4 specialties
6-WEEK DIARY STUDY — 22 clinicians
AUDIT LOG ANALYSIS — 8 weeks of data
3-CLINIC FIELD TRIAL — 60 days
01
SOAP-aligned section order

Reordered sections to mirror SOAP (Subjective → Objective → Assessment → Plan). Clinicians stopped scrolling. Time-to-first-field dropped from 22 to 6 seconds.

Trade-off → required updating every audit template downstream. 4 months of compliance work, validated case-by-case.
02
Inline non-blocking validation

Replaced modal errors with gentle inline cues. The form never blocks the clinician mid-thought. Errors surface at submit, with a clear path to fix.

Outcome → "feels less like a form" was the most common verbatim from field trial.
03
Smart defaults at the field, not in settings

When a field had a likely value (based on patient history, specialty, or previous chart), surfaced it as a one-tap accept inline. No settings menu required.

Trade-off → required a per-specialty config, which engineering pushed back on. Won by showing the time savings in the field trial.

(09) — Reflection

What I'd do
differently.

What worked
Shadowing > surveys

The audit-vs-encounter ordering insight was visible in 5 minutes of shadowing and invisible in 6 weeks of survey data. Be in the room.

Compliance as a co-author

Bringing the compliance officer into design reviews — not just sign-off — surfaced constraints early and produced better designs faster.

Field trial before rollout

The 3-clinic trial caught two regressions and validated the metric movement. Worth every week.

What I'd do differently
Earlier engagement with EHR vendors

Some integration constraints surfaced late. Earlier vendor conversations could have widened the design space.

Better baseline instrumentation

The 8.2 min baseline came from log analysis, not a clean experiment. Cleaner instrumentation would have made the result harder to dispute.

Plan for the off-floor charting

A non-trivial fraction of charting happens at home, late at night. I designed for the floor and treated home-charting as out of scope. It shouldn't have been.

"The clinician finishes their day at 6, not 9. That's the metric. Everything else is downstream."
