(02) — Context

What Arbolus
actually does.

Arbolus is a B2B expert network platform — it uses ML and NLP to surface domain experts, match them to client projects, and facilitate knowledge sessions. Think: a marketplace for human insight, with AI doing the heavy lifting on discovery and quality.

The platform serves two sides: expert users — consultants, operators, academics — and client users — strategy teams, PE firms, corporate development. Both sides have fundamentally different needs, mental models, and definitions of trust.

(03) — The Problem

The platform knew
too much and showed
all of it.

The original platform had accumulated two years of feature additions with no coherent system behind them. Each team had shipped independently. The result: a product that was technically capable but experientially hostile.

Three patterns kept surfacing in user sessions — and none of them were about missing features.

3
Core failure modes discovered
before a single screen was redesigned
Failure mode 01
Cognitive overload

Expert profiles surfaced 40+ data points with no hierarchy. Users couldn't find signal through the noise. Session booking rates were a fraction of what they should be.

Failure mode 02
Broken trust signals

Compliance and vetting flows were buried. Clients couldn't quickly assess expert credibility. Deal cycles stretched because sales had to manually validate what the UI should've shown.

Failure mode 03
Zero design language

12 different button styles. 3 modal patterns. No token system. Every new feature was a one-off. Developer velocity slowed. Inconsistency eroded confidence in the product.

"We're losing deals not because of the product, but because people can't see the product."

— Sales feedback, Q1 2023

Deep Dive 01
Platform Redesign

From cluttered
to conversion.

The expert discovery flow was the company's primary revenue lever. Clients who found and booked experts converted to paid. Clients who didn't — churned without ever realising the platform's value.

The flow was technically functional but experientially broken: too many steps, unclear trust signals, and expert profiles that buried the most important information.

The research
12 USER INTERVIEWS
6 client-side · 6 expert-side

80+ SESSION REPLAYS
Booking attempt analysis

FUNNEL ANALYSIS
Where drop-off actually happened

"Trust is established before the first message. The profile page was doing the work of a CV, not a pitch."

— Key insight from user sessions
01
Lead with relevance, not credentials

Restructured expert profiles to show: why this expert for this project (AI-matched reasons) before education and work history. Clients were abandoning at the profile view — not at checkout. The mental model mismatch was the real problem.

Trade-off → Required ML team to expose matching rationale via API. 2 sprints engineering cost. Conversion gain: significant.
02
Collapse compliance into context

Instead of a separate "Verified" badge page, surfaced vetting signals inline — at the moment of decision, not before it. Trust signals work best when they appear exactly when trust is needed.

Trade-off → Every copy variation required legal review. Slowed iteration, improved trust measurably.
03
Reduce booking to 3 steps

Original: 7-step booking flow with branching for compliance. New: 3-step with compliance handled asynchronously post-booking. The friction wasn't regulatory — it was sequencing. Moving compliance out of the critical path changed the feel entirely.

Trade-off → Required a product policy change, not just a design change. This was the hardest internal sell.

(07) — Design System

The invisible work that
made everything else
possible.

The design system wasn't a separate project — it was a survival tool. Without it, every new screen meant reconciling 3 different button patterns and guessing which spacing scale engineering was actually using.

Built iteratively alongside feature work, starting with what hurt most: typography, colour, and spacing tokens. The grid system was rebuilt from scratch — previous grid: inconsistent, undocumented. New grid: 12-column, 8px base unit, 3 breakpoints.

(10) — Reflection

What I'd do
differently.

What worked
Starting with the system, not the screens

Without the grid rebuild and token foundation, everything downstream would've been slower and messier. The compounding value of system work is consistently underrated.

Treating sales as a research channel

Client-facing teams carry qualitative signal that formal research doesn't always reach. That monthly loop became one of the most efficient feedback mechanisms I built.

Making design decisions legible

Framing decisions as product bets with hypotheses changed the quality of stakeholder conversations — and eventually changed my seat at the table.

What I'd do differently
Instrument before launching, not after

Several impact claims are directional because analytics weren't in place at baseline. I'd push harder for instrumentation as a pre-launch prerequisite, not a post-ship task.

Earlier compliance involvement

I treated legal review as a gate rather than a collaborator. Earlier loops would've prevented late-stage design changes — and probably unlocked better patterns.

More structured A/B testing

The conversion result is directional. With more structured testing infrastructure, I'd have harder numbers — and a stronger case for the next initiative.
